## Pharmaceuticals in Water: Sources, Fate and Effects (Rodriguez-Mozaz & Weinberg, Environmental Health Perspectives 2010) (2680 characters)

This reading is the article Pharmaceuticals in Water: Sources, Fate and Effects, by Rodriguez-Mozaz and Weinberg, published in Environmental Health Perspectives in 2010. It was assigned for October ninth and discussed on October thirteenth with the discussion guide. The assigned portion runs from the beginning up to the section on best management practices. On October thirteenth she said, "that was the end of the paper that you didn't read."

Her lecture drew these points from it. First, the sources. The body metabolizes only part of a prescribed drug, so unchanged drug and metabolites leave in human waste and travel through wastewater into streams, lakes, and the water that drinking water plants use. Wastewater treatment plants are the main entry point, because their treatment steps were not designed to remove pharmaceuticals. Some drugs collect in biosolids sold as fertilizer, and farm animals get drugs in feed. She said, "the water from Flushing gets treated a bit, but right now the current mechanisms don't get all the drugs out of it before it goes back into your streams and rivers and lakes." She asked, "So is that a source for humans or animals? Both, there you go." Second, the routes of exposure. Humans take the drugs in mainly by mouth through drinking water. Aquatic animals live in the receiving water, so they are exposed constantly. Third, the dose contrast. Human exposure is far below a therapeutic dose, while wildlife is exposed continuously. Fourth, the wildlife effects. She said, "you'll start seeing populations of frogs and some fish and alligators changing from being like half male, half female to more female in those places where estrogen levels are higher, we see that." Fifth, the five principles of toxicology sit in the assigned part. She said, "the part of the paper I had you read was really where you're going to find those principles of toxicology."

## Chapter 57: Heavy Metal Intoxication & Chelators (2658 characters)

This reading is Chapter 57, Heavy Metal Intoxication and Chelators, from Katzung's Basic and Clinical Pharmacology, sixteenth edition. It belongs to the Toxic Responses and Treating Poisonings lectures of October thirteenth and fourteenth, but the chelator slides were not reached on October fourteenth and were deferred to Monday, October nineteenth. Doctor Smith has not discussed this chapter in class yet. Her rule is that readings are tested as discussed.

The chapter has two parts. The first covers the toxicology of lead, arsenic and mercury. The second covers the pharmacology of chelators, which are drugs used to prevent or reverse the toxic effects of a heavy metal on an enzyme or other cellular target, or to accelerate elimination of the metal from the body. The card points to the chelator part. First, the opening of the Pharmacology of Chelators section. A chelating agent contains coordinating atoms, usually oxygen, sulfur or nitrogen, that donate a pair of electrons to a metal ion. Depending on the number of metal ligand bonds, the complex is mono, bi, or polydentate. This is the content of her Inactivation of Poisons: Heavy Metal Chelators slide. Second, Figure 57 dash 1, the lead edetate chelate with lead held in five heterocyclic rings, which matches her Structure of chelators slide, and Figure 57 dash 2 with the structures of dimercaprol, succimer, penicillamine, unithiol and deferoxamine. Third, the agents on her Example Chelating Agents slide. Dimercaprol, called B A L for British anti-lewisite, is a ten percent solution in peanut oil given intramuscularly, approved for acute arsenic and inorganic mercury poisoning and for severe lead poisoning with E D T A. Succimer, called D M S A, is the water soluble oral analog of dimercaprol used for lead in children with blood lead above 45 micrograms per deciliter; her slide says it is taking the place of dimercaprol. Edetate calcium disodium, called E D T A, is the intravenous chelator of extracellular lead, and only the calcium disodium salt is used, to prevent depletion of calcium. Deferoxamine is the parenteral chelator of choice for iron poisoning and turns the urine orange red. Deferasirox is the oral iron chelator. Penicillamine chelates copper in Wilson disease. Prussian blue binds radioactive cesium and thallium. Fourth, the treatment subsections that say which chelator goes with which metal, and metal redistribution by chelators, such as dimercaprol moving mercury and arsenic to the brain.

Re-read first the opening of the Pharmacology of Chelators section with Figure 57 dash 1, then the Dimercaprol, Succimer and Edetate Calcium Disodium sections.

If this is the intended chapter, the card points to these sections. First, the definition. Medication adherence, following the World Health Organization, is the extent to which an individual's medication taking behavior aligns with the recommendations from healthcare providers, in terms of dose, route, frequency and duration. The estimated adherence rate for chronic illnesses in developed countries is around fifty percent. Nonadherence can be intentional or unintentional. Adherent patients have fewer hospitalizations and emergency department visits and lower costs. Second, direct methods of measuring adherence: bioassays and biomarkers in blood or urine, and direct observation. These are accurate but intrusive, costly, and unable to reveal usage patterns. Third, indirect methods: self report surveys, the most widely adopted method, which can overestimate adherence; pharmacy refill measures, the medication possession ratio and the proportion of days covered; pill counts, which do not apply to as needed medications; clinical response; and the Medication Events Monitoring System, a container closure that logs each opening. Fourth, the World Health Organization's five interacting dimensions of adherence: social and economic factors, healthcare team and system related factors, condition related factors, therapy related factors, and patient related factors. Therapy related factors include frequency and route of administration, quantity of daily dosage, size of solid oral dosage forms, and duration of infusions. Fifth, strategies for improving adherence: the SIMPLE strategy of simplifying the regimen, imparting knowledge, modifying beliefs, patient communication, leaving the bias, and evaluating adherence; the pharmacist's role in patient education, medication synchronization and team based care; and the RULE acronym for motivational interviewing.
